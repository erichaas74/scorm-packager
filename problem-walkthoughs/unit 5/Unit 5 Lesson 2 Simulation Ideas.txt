Unit 5 Lesson 2: Sound, Resonance, Harmonics, and Doppler Effect


Teacher Simulation Walkthrough Ideas


Part 1: Sound as a Mechanical Wave
Learning goal: Explain how vibrating sources create longitudinal sound waves and how sound travels through different media.


Simulation ideas
• Vibrating-source model: Show a tuning fork or speaker creating alternating compressions and rarefactions.
• Particle-motion view: Track air particles moving back and forth parallel to the direction the sound travels.
• Vacuum test: Gradually remove particles and show the sound weakening and disappearing.
• Medium comparison: Race the same sound pulse through a solid, liquid, and gas.
• Temperature comparison: Send sound through hot, room-temperature, and cold air and compare arrival times.
• Medium-property comparison: Show that sound speed depends on how efficiently a medium transfers motion, not density alone.
• Spherical wavefront model: Animate expanding compression rings or spheres from a point source.
• Wavelength in a longitudinal wave: Freeze the model and measure between adjacent compressions.
• Intensity-distance model: Move a detector away from a speaker and show energy spreading over a larger spherical area.
• Inverse-square reveal: Double the distance and show intensity becoming one-fourth rather than one-half.
• Sound behavior station: Demonstrate reflection, refraction, diffraction, and interference using the same sound source.
• Anechoic chamber comparison: Compare a clap in a normal room with a room that absorbs reflections.
• Energy-transfer demonstration: Use particles or lightweight objects to show that sound transfers energy without transporting matter across the room.


Prediction and misconception questions
• Does sound travel faster in empty space because nothing blocks it? Reveal that sound cannot travel without a medium.
• Are sound waves transverse because they are commonly drawn as sine waves? Reveal longitudinal particle motion.
• Does the air travel from the speaker to the listener? Reveal that particles oscillate locally while energy travels.
• Does sound always travel faster in a denser medium? Reveal that elasticity and particle interactions also matter.
• Will sound reach you sooner on a hot day or a cold day? Reveal faster travel in warmer air.
• If you double your distance from a speaker, is the intensity cut in half? Reveal that it becomes one-fourth.
• Does a sound wave carry matter across the room? Reveal that the medium returns to its original position.


Part 2: Frequency, Pitch, Resonance, and Harmonics
Learning goal: Connect frequency, amplitude, pitch, loudness, resonance, standing waves, harmonics, and timbre.


Simulation ideas
• Pitch and loudness controls: Change amplitude and frequency independently so students separate loudness from pitch.
• Frequency-range sweep: Move from infrasonic through audible to ultrasonic frequencies and show examples of animals and technologies that detect each range.
• Hearing-range comparison: Compare a younger listener, older listener, and selected animals.
• Human-hearing sensitivity map: Show that hearing depends on both frequency and intensity.
• Decibel-scale reveal: Increase from 20 dB to 60 dB and show a 10,000-times increase in intensity.
• Ear pathway model: Follow vibrations through the eardrum, middle-ear bones, cochlea, hair cells, and auditory nerve.
• Cochlea frequency map: Change pitch and highlight which region of the basilar membrane responds.
• Resonance model: Drive an oscillator at different frequencies and reveal the large response near its natural frequency.
• Standing-wave builder: Show an incident wave and reflected wave combining into a standing-wave pattern.
• Particle tracker on standing wave: Highlight stationary nodes and strongly oscillating antinodes.
• String harmonics: Display the first four harmonics while changing string length or tension.
• Open-open pipe model: Show antinodes at both ends and the allowed harmonic patterns.
• Open-closed pipe model: Show a node at the closed end, an antinode at the open end, and the allowed odd harmonics.
• Harmonic-fit challenge: Attempt to place different patterns inside open and closed pipes and reveal which fit.
• Rubens tube visualization: Use flame height to make nodes and antinodes visible.
• Beat generator: Combine two nearby frequencies and display alternating constructive and destructive interference.
• Instrument tuner: Adjust one frequency until beats slow and disappear when the frequencies match.
• Timbre builder: Add harmonics one at a time and show how the waveform and sound quality change.
• Instrument comparison: Compare a flute and violin playing the same pitch and volume but with different harmonic mixtures.
• Period and wavelength calculator: Provide frequency and sound speed, then reveal period and spacing between compressions.
• Temperature-wavelength connection: Keep frequency fixed while changing air temperature and reveal the wavelength change.


Prediction and misconception questions
• Does blowing harder into an instrument always make the pitch higher? Reveal that it usually increases amplitude and loudness, not the fundamental pitch.
• Does greater amplitude mean greater frequency? Reveal that amplitude and frequency are independent.
• Are loudness and pitch the same property? Reveal amplitude controls loudness while frequency controls pitch.
• Is a 60 dB sound only three times as intense as a 20 dB sound? Reveal the logarithmic scale and 10,000-times intensity difference.
• Is a standing wave actually standing still? Reveal that particles continue oscillating except at nodes.
• Are nodes places where no wave energy exists anywhere? Reveal zero displacement at that location while energy remains in the standing-wave system.
• Can an open-closed pipe produce every harmonic? Reveal that its basic pattern supports odd harmonics.
• Why do a flute and violin sound different on the same note? Reveal different harmonic mixtures and timbre.
• If two frequencies are nearly equal, do they produce a new steady pitch? Reveal periodic loud-soft beats.
• When the beats disappear during tuning, did the sound stop? Reveal that the frequencies matched.
• Can humans hear every frequency if it is loud enough? Reveal biological limits of the ear.


Part 3: Doppler Effect Evidence
Learning goal: Use wavefront evidence to explain changes in observed frequency and pitch caused by relative motion.


Simulation ideas
• Moving ambulance model: Show wavefronts compressing ahead of the source and spreading behind it.
• Three-observer comparison: Place observers ahead of, beside, and behind a moving source and compare detected frequency.
• Emitted-versus-observed display: Keep source frequency visible while showing different detected frequencies.
• Moving-source versus moving-observer comparison: Demonstrate how both produce a Doppler shift through relative motion.
• Shared-motion test: Move the source and observer together at the same velocity and show little or no shift between them.
• Approach-and-pass sequence: Let students hear and see the pitch change as a vehicle approaches, passes, and recedes.
• Speed-of-sound buildup: Increase aircraft speed until wavefronts pile up into a pressure front.
• Mach-cone model: Animate the shock cone trailing a supersonic aircraft.
• Continuous sonic-boom demonstration: Move the aircraft past several observers and show each hearing the boom when the cone reaches them.
• Small sonic-boom examples: Animate a bullet, whip tip, or towel tip exceeding the speed of sound.
• Evidence graph: Plot detected frequency against relative velocity and connect the graph to wavefront spacing.


Prediction and misconception questions
• Is an approaching ambulance actually producing a higher source frequency? Reveal that the emitted frequency stays constant while the observed frequency changes.
• Does the Doppler effect change wave speed? Reveal that wave speed remains set by the medium.
• Does the pitch change only because the source becomes louder as it approaches? Reveal compressed wavefronts and higher detected frequency.
• Does an observer beside the path hear the same shift as an observer directly ahead? Reveal that the radial component of relative motion matters.
• If the source and observer move together at the same velocity, must there be a Doppler shift? Reveal that relative motion between them determines the shift.
• Is a sonic boom produced only at the instant an aircraft crosses the sound barrier? Reveal a continuous shock cone during supersonic travel.
• Does the pilot hear a sonic boom continuously inside the aircraft? Reveal that the boom is heard when the shock front passes an observer.
• Must an object be a speaker to create a sonic boom? Reveal that any object moving supersonically can generate shock waves.


Recommended teacher walkthrough sequence
1. Start with a vibrating source and track particle motion through the medium.
2. Remove the medium to show why sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
3. Race sound through different media and temperatures.
4. Move a detector away from a point source and reveal the inverse-square intensity change.
5. Change amplitude and frequency separately to distinguish loudness from pitch.
6. Sweep through infrasonic, audible, and ultrasonic frequencies.
7. Build a standing wave and identify nodes and antinodes.
8. Compare harmonics on a string, open pipe, and closed pipe.
9. Add harmonics to demonstrate timbre.
10. Generate beats and tune two frequencies until the beats disappear.
11. Move a sound source past observers to demonstrate the Doppler effect.
12. Increase the source to supersonic speed and reveal the continuous Mach cone.


Highest-priority counterintuitive reveals
• Sound cannot travel through empty space.
• Sound particles oscillate locally rather than traveling with the wave.
• Louder sound does not automatically mean higher pitch.
• Doubling distance reduces point-source intensity to one-fourth.
• Decibels use a logarithmic rather than linear scale.
• Standing waves contain moving particles even though the pattern appears stationary.
• Open-closed pipes do not support the same harmonic sequence as open-open pipes.
• Beats disappear when frequencies match, not when the sounds disappear.
• Doppler shift changes observed frequency, not source frequency or wave speed.
• Sonic booms continue as long as an object remains supersonic.
