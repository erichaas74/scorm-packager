Unit 5 Lesson 5: Wave-Particle Duality and Nuclear Models


Teacher Simulation Walkthrough Ideas


Part 1: Light as Wave and Particle
Learning goal: Use evidence to explain when wave and particle models of light are most useful.


Simulation ideas
• Double-slit model: Send light through two slits and reveal bright and dark interference bands.
• Single-photon buildup: Send photons one at a time and watch an interference pattern gradually form.
• Photoelectric-effect model: Change light frequency and intensity independently while tracking emitted electrons.
• Threshold-frequency test: Increase brightness below the threshold and show no electrons released; use dim higher-frequency light and release electrons.
• Wave-versus-particle evidence sorter: Match interference, diffraction, polarization, and photoelectric evidence to the model that best explains it.
• Polarizer model: Rotate two polarizing filters and compare transmitted brightness.
• Light Doppler model: Move a source toward or away from an observer and shift absorption lines toward blue or red while light speed remains constant.
• Extreme-speed color shift: Show how fast an observer would need to move to shift red light toward green.
• RGB additive mixing: Overlap red, green, and blue light to form secondary colors and white.
• Subtractive color model: Use cyan, magenta, and yellow pigments or filters to absorb selected wavelengths.
• Colored-object lighting test: Shine different colored lights on colored surfaces and predict the observed color.
• Cone-response model: Show how three human cone types combine responses to produce perceived color.
• Human-versus-bird color vision: Compare three-cone and four-cone vision, including ultraviolet sensitivity.


Prediction and misconception questions
• Does light switch back and forth between being a wave and a particle? Reveal that both models describe different observed behaviors.
• Does a single photon simply travel through one slit in the same way as a tiny ball? Reveal the single-photon interference buildup.
• Will brighter low-frequency light eventually eject electrons? Reveal that frequency must exceed the threshold.
• Does greater brightness give each photon more energy? Reveal that brightness mainly changes photon number.
• Does an approaching light source increase light speed? Reveal a frequency shift while measured light speed stays constant.
• Does blue shift mean the source becomes physically blue? Reveal shifted spectral lines caused by motion.
• Do colored objects always look like their normal color? Reveal that appearance depends on both illumination and reflected wavelengths.
• Will a magenta object look magenta under green light? Reveal that it can appear dark because it absorbs green.
• Do screens and printers create colors the same way? Reveal additive versus subtractive color mixing.


Part 2: Nuclear Processes and Energy Release
Learning goal: Model changes in nuclei and energy release during radioactive decay, fission, and fusion.


Simulation ideas
• Nuclear-change tracker: Display proton number, neutron number, element identity, and emitted radiation before and after each process.
• Alpha-decay model: Remove two protons and two neutrons and reveal the new element.
• Beta-decay model: Convert a neutron to a proton or a proton to a neutron while emitting the appropriate particles.
• Gamma-emission model: Lower the nucleus’s energy without changing proton or neutron count.
• Fission model: Split a heavy nucleus, release neutrons and energy, and optionally trigger a chain reaction.
• Fusion model: Combine light nuclei, show the mass difference, and represent the released energy.
• Half-life population model: Run repeated decay intervals and show that individual decay times are unpredictable but population trends are predictable.
• Energy-scale comparison: Compare nuclear energy release with chemical and mechanical processes qualitatively.
• Control-versus-runaway fission: Compare neutron absorption in a reactor with an uncontrolled chain reaction.


Prediction and misconception questions
• Does radioactive decay make matter disappear? Reveal daughter nuclei, emitted particles, and transferred energy.
• Does gamma decay change the element? Reveal that it changes nuclear energy but not proton number.
• Can scientists predict exactly when one unstable nucleus will decay? Reveal probabilistic individual behavior and predictable large-sample patterns.
• Is half-life the time required for every nucleus to decay? Reveal repeated halving rather than complete disappearance.
• Are fission and fusion the same process because both release energy? Reveal splitting versus combining nuclei.
• Does nuclear energy violate conservation of energy? Reveal conversion involving mass-energy and changes in nuclear binding.
• Can one emitted neutron always sustain a chain reaction? Reveal neutron escape, absorption, and critical conditions.


Part 3: Model Evidence for Modern Physics
Learning goal: Evaluate evidence and select models that best explain modern-physics observations.


Simulation ideas
• Evidence station: Compare double-slit interference, photoelectric effect, emission spectra, absorption spectra, and radioactive decay.
• Spectral-line motion detector: Shift a known absorption pattern and infer whether a star is approaching or receding.
• Scientific-model timeline: Compare how improved evidence led from classical wave models to photon and nuclear models.
• Same-color different-spectrum model: Show different wavelength mixtures that produce similar cone responses and perceived color.
• Sun-energy model: Connect fusion in the Sun to radiation that eventually reaches Earth.
• Model-limit challenge: Ask which model explains each observation and where that model is insufficient.
• Optional relativity extension: Compare simultaneity from train and ground reference frames.
• Optional GPS clock extension: Show special- and general-relativity clock effects combining in satellite timing.


Prediction and misconception questions
• Does one model have to explain every property of light equally well? Reveal that models have specific useful domains.
• Is an interference pattern proof that light can never act like particles? Reveal discrete photon detections forming a wave-like distribution.
• Are absorption lines missing because a star failed to produce those colors? Reveal selective absorption by intervening matter.
• Can two physically different spectra look like the same color to humans? Reveal similar cone stimulation.
• Is scientific model change evidence that earlier science was useless? Reveal how models remain useful within their limits and improve with evidence.
• Is time the same for every observer? Optional reveal using relativity and reference frames.


Recommended teacher walkthrough sequence
1. Run a double-slit experiment with a bright beam.
2. Repeat with single photons and build the same pattern over time.
3. Test the photoelectric effect by changing intensity and frequency separately.
4. Rotate polarizers to add another wave-model observation.
5. Demonstrate red and blue shifts with spectral lines.
6. Mix RGB light and compare it with CMY pigment mixing.
7. Model alpha, beta, gamma, fission, and fusion while tracking nuclear composition.
8. Run a half-life population model.
9. Sort evidence by the model that best explains it.


Highest-priority counterintuitive reveals
• Individual photon impacts can build a wave-interference pattern.
• Low-frequency light may fail to eject electrons regardless of brightness.
• Light Doppler shifts frequency, not the measured speed of light.
• An object’s observed color depends on both its surface and the illuminating light.
• Radioactive decay changes nuclei rather than making matter vanish.
• Half-life predicts populations, not the exact decay time of one nucleus.
• Different scientific models can be useful for different observations.
