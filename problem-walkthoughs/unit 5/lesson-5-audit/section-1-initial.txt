Is Light a Wave, a Particle, or Both?

For centuries, scientists have debated the true nature of light. Is it a wave, a particle, or something else entirely? Understanding how light behaves isn’t just a question for physicists—it shapes the technology we use every day, from cameras to solar panels. In this lesson, you’ll explore the evidence that has led to our modern understanding of light’s dual nature and see how scientific models help us make sense of the world.

In this lesson, you’ll develop a toolkit for analyzing how light behaves in different situations. By the end, you’ll be able to connect real experimental evidence to the models scientists use.

Evidence for Wave-Particle Duality

How do we know that light behaves as both a wave and a particle? In this section, you’ll examine key experiments and observations. You’ll test different models against real phenomena and see which explanations hold up to the evidence.

The double-slit experiment is a classic demonstration of light’s wave-like behavior. When a beam of light passes through two narrow, closely spaced slits, it creates a pattern of alternating bright and dark bands on a screen. This interference pattern is difficult to explain if light is made only of particles, but it makes perfect sense if light behaves as a wave—waves from each slit overlap and combine, creating regions of constructive and destructive interference.

The Photoelectric Effect: Particle Evidence

While interference patterns support the wave model, some experiments can only be explained if light acts as particles. The photoelectric effect is a key example: when light shines on certain metals, it can eject electrons from the surface—but only if the light’s frequency is high enough. This phenomenon led to the idea of photons: particles of light with energy determined by their frequency.

Introduction to the Photoelectric Effect Simulation

This lesson explores the photoelectric effect using a simulation. You will learn how changing the frequency and intensity of light affects the emission of electrons from a metal surface.

START
2 of 5
Step 1
Shining Light on Metal

Light of a chosen frequency and intensity is directed at a metal surface. You can adjust both the frequency (color) and the brightness (intensity) of the light.

3 of 5
Step 2
Observing Electron Emission

If the light’s frequency is above a certain threshold, electrons are ejected from the metal. If the frequency is too low, no electrons are emitted, no matter how bright the light is.

4 of 5
Step 3
Changing Intensity

Increasing the intensity (brightness) of high-frequency light causes more electrons to be emitted, but does not change their energy. For low-frequency light, increasing intensity still does not cause any electrons to be released.

5 of 5
Summary of the Photoelectric Effect

The photoelectric effect shows that light energy comes in packets (photons), and only photons with enough energy (high enough frequency) can eject electrons. This supports the particle model of light.

The energy of each photon depends on its frequency, not its intensity. Increasing the frequency of light increases the energy of each photon, making it more likely to eject electrons from a metal. Increasing the intensity means more photons are present, but each photon still has the same energy. That’s why only high-frequency light can cause the photoelectric effect, regardless of how bright the light is.

When you increase the frequency of light (for example, from red to blue), each photon carries more energy. If the frequency is high enough, photons can transfer enough energy to electrons in a metal to knock them free. This is why ultraviolet light can cause the photoelectric effect, while red light cannot, no matter how bright it is.

Increasing frequency does not change the number of photons, but it does make each photon more energetic. The higher the frequency, the more energy each photon has, and the more likely it is to eject electrons from the metal surface.

DOPPLER SHIFT
COLOR & MIXING

The Doppler shift occurs when a source of light moves toward or away from an observer. If the source moves toward you, the light’s frequency appears higher (blue shift); if it moves away, the frequency appears lower (red shift). This effect is used in astronomy to measure the motion of stars and galaxies.

Importantly, the speed of light remains constant, even as the observed frequency changes. The color shift is due to changes in frequency, not speed.

Worked example: Can a photon eject an electron?

A hypothetical surface has a work function of 2.00 eV. Compare 500 nm light with 700 nm light, using E ≈ 1240/λ when wavelength is in nanometres and energy is in electronvolts.

START
2 of 6
Step 1
Calculate the incident photon energy

Use the energy of one photon. The work function is the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from this surface.

3 of 6
Step 2
Compare energy with the threshold

The photon has more energy than the work function. Emission is possible; the remaining energy can become electron kinetic energy.

4 of 6
Step 3
Find maximum kinetic energy

Subtract the energy needed to remove the electron. This gives maximum kinetic energy, not the energy of every emitted electron.

5 of 6
Step 4
Test a longer wavelength

At 700 nm, each photon has too little energy. Increasing photon flux still does not cause emission in this introductory single-photon model. The threshold depends on the material.

6 of 6
Check the complete result

A 500 nm photon can produce an electron with up to 0.48 eV of kinetic energy here. A 700 nm photon cannot overcome this surface’s work function.
