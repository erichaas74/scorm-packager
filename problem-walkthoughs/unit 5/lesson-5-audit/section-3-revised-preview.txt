Evidence Stations: Which Model Explains What?

Different experiments and observations support different scientific models. Some evidence points clearly to the wave model, others to the particle or nuclear model, and sometimes more than one model is needed. Being able to match evidence to the right model is a crucial scientific skill.

Choose the model for the question

A classical wave model is useful for interference and polarization. A photon model explains discrete energy exchanges. Quantum theory accounts for both types of behavior; light does not switch between two classical identities. A limitation is a prediction the model cannot make accurately, not merely a difficult experimental condition.

A model is useful because it explains certain evidence—not because it explains everything.

Spectral Lines: What Can They Tell Us?

When scientists look at the light from stars or gases, they see patterns of dark or bright lines called spectra. These lines reveal information about the elements present and whether the source is moving toward or away from us. Spectral evidence is a powerful tool for testing and selecting scientific models.

Walkthrough: Explain spectral lines and their shifts

Use two linked models: quantized atomic energy levels explain line patterns, and a Doppler model relates a shifted pattern to radial motion.

START
2 of 6
Step 1
Connect a line to an energy difference

An atom absorbs a photon when the photon energy matches an allowed upward transition. A downward transition emits a photon with that energy. In a gas, these allowed differences produce characteristic absorption or emission lines.

3 of 6
Step 2
Match the same pattern

Compare several lines in a laboratory reference with the observed source. First identify a matching pattern. A single dark line by itself is weak evidence for a particular element.

4 of 6
Step 3
Infer radial motion under the Doppler model

A pattern shifted to shorter wavelengths is blueshifted; in this model the source approaches along the line of sight. A longer-wavelength shift indicates recession. Observed frequency and wavelength change, but light still travels at c in vacuum. No radial shift does not rule out sideways motion.

5 of 6
Step 4
Check the fractional shift

Use the same fractional shift for all matched lines. If a 500 nm line appears at 505 nm, z = 0.010; a 600 nm line should then appear at 606 nm. Other causes of shifts require more advanced models, so state the Doppler assumption.

6 of 6
Check your understanding

Atomic energy levels explain why the lines exist. Comparing their wavelengths with laboratory values supplies evidence about motion under the Doppler model.

Worked example: Calculate a spectral shift

A spectral line with a rest wavelength of 500 nm is observed at 505 nm. Find the fractional shift and estimate radial speed using the low-speed Doppler approximation.

START
2 of 6
Step 1
Compare observed and rest wavelengths

The observed wavelength is longer. This is a redshift in the simple motion model. Keep both wavelengths in the same units.

3 of 6
Step 2
Calculate the fractional shift

Divide the change by the rest wavelength. The nanometre units cancel, so redshift z is dimensionless.

4 of 6
Step 3
Estimate radial speed

For speeds small compared with light speed, radial speed is approximately c times z. Use c ≈ 300,000 km/s. The positive sign indicates recession in this convention.

5 of 6
Step 4
Check a second spectral line

All lines share the same fractional shift in this model. A rest line at 600 nm moves to 606 nm, not 605 nm. No detected shift would mean no detected radial motion, not necessarily no sideways motion.

6 of 6
Check the complete result

The source is receding at approximately 3,000 km/s along the line of sight. The approximation is appropriate for this small fractional shift.

Walkthrough: Connect fusion to the sunlight we observe

Different evidence answers different questions about the Sun. Follow the energy and identify which model is useful at each stage.

START
2 of 7
Step 1
Identify the nuclear source

The proton–proton chain in the core converts hydrogen into helium through intermediate nuclei. Nuclear reaction models explain the energy release; solar neutrinos provide evidence of reactions in the core.

3 of 7
Step 2
Share energy with the hot plasma

Reaction products transfer energy to surrounding particles and radiation. Much of the released energy heats the plasma; neutrinos interact weakly and escape. A visible photon arriving at Earth is not simply an unchanged gamma photon flying straight from the core.

4 of 7
Step 3
Transport energy through the layers

In the radiative interior, repeated absorption, emission, and scattering transfer energy outward. In the convection zone, moving plasma also transports energy. Photons and moving matter have distinct roles; light does not change from a classical particle into a classical wave between regions.

5 of 7
Step 4
Form the observed spectrum

Light escapes near the photosphere. Cooler gas absorbs particular photon energies, producing dark absorption lines. Quantized atomic energy levels explain the line pattern, while the broader spectrum carries information about temperature.

6 of 7
Step 5
Connect observations to models

Telescopes and detectors measure the arriving light. Wave models explain interference and diffraction in optics; photon models explain discrete energy detection. Spectral lines identify elements in the line-forming gas, while neutrinos probe the core. No single observation alone establishes the whole chain.

Check your understanding

Condition or limitation?

Click to flip

High temperature and adequate confinement are conditions needed for fusion. A limitation is something a model does not explain or predict accurately.

Click to flip

What does gamma emission change?

Click to flip

The nuclear energy state changes. Proton and neutron counts remain unchanged, so the element and isotope stay the same.

Click to flip

How strong is a spectral match?

Click to flip

Compare several characteristic lines with laboratory wavelengths. A consistent fractional shift supports a Doppler interpretation of radial motion; it does not measure sideways motion.

Click to flip

What does E = hf predict?

Click to flip

The energy of one photon. In ordinary single-photon photoemission, increasing intensity below the surface threshold cannot compensate for insufficient energy per photon.

Click to flip

What does E = Δmc² predict?

Click to flip

The energy corresponding to a rest-mass difference. For an energy-releasing reaction, Δm is the total reactant rest mass minus the total product rest mass. Total mass-energy is conserved.
