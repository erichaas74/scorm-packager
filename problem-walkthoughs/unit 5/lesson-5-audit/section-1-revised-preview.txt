The Photoelectric Effect: Particle Evidence

In the photoelectric effect, a surface emits electrons when individual photons transfer enough energy to remove them. The material sets the threshold. We use the introductory single-photon model at ordinary light intensities.

Step 1
Compare one photon with the barrier

The work function is the minimum energy needed to remove an electron. The photon’s energy depends on frequency, not on the number of other photons present.

3 of 5
Step 2
Decide whether an electron can escape

Below this surface’s threshold, a photon cannot remove an electron. Above threshold, emission is possible and the remaining energy can become electron kinetic energy.

4 of 5
Step 3
Predict the effect of brighter light

Keep the frequency above threshold fixed. Greater intensity means more incident photons per second per unit area, usually producing more emitted electrons. The maximum electron kinetic energy stays the same.

Changing the material changes the threshold. Changing frequency changes energy per photon. Changing intensity at fixed frequency changes photon arrival rate.

Frequency vs. Intensity

Explore each tab to compare how changing frequency or intensity affects photon energy and electron emission.

INCREASING FREQUENCY
INCREASING INTENSITY

Higher frequency means more energy per photon: E = hf. Emission requires enough energy to overcome the material’s work function. Red light can eject electrons from a sufficiently low-work-function surface; ultraviolet light is not a universal requirement.

Specify what stays fixed: at fixed photon arrival rate, higher frequency increases intensity; at fixed intensity, higher frequency means fewer photons arriving per second per unit area.
