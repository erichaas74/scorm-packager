Inside the Atom: Nuclei and Isotopes

Atomic number Z counts protons and identifies the element. Mass number A counts protons plus neutrons. Isotopes have the same Z but different neutron counts. Nuclear stability is determined from evidence about the isotope; it cannot be inferred just by looking at a nucleus diagram.

Radioactive Decay: How Atoms Transform

Unstable nuclei don’t stay the same forever. To become more stable, they can change by releasing particles or energy—a process called radioactive decay. Each type of decay has its own signature, changing the nucleus in specific ways and sometimes even transforming one element into another.

Half-Life: Predicting Decay in Populations

Each unstable nucleus has a probability of decaying. Half-life is the time for the expected number of undecayed parent nuclei to fall by half. Large populations closely follow this pattern; small samples fluctuate. The decay products remain as other nuclei and emitted particles.

Fission and Fusion: Two Ways to Release Nuclear Energy

Fission splits a heavy nucleus; fusion combines light nuclei. Energy is released when the products have less total rest mass than the reactants. The difference appears as energy of motion and radiation, so total mass-energy is conserved.

Walkthrough: Track a fission chain reaction

Follow one possible neutron-induced fission of uranium-235. Other fission products are possible.

START
2 of 6
Step 1
Absorb a neutron

Uranium-235 absorbs a neutron and forms excited uranium-236. The extra neutron increases A from 235 to 236; Z remains 92. This example is induced fission, although spontaneous fission also exists.

3 of 6
Step 2
Balance the fission products

One possible outcome produces barium-141, krypton-92, and three free neutrons. Nucleon counts balance: 236 = 141 + 92 + 3. Proton counts balance: 92 = 56 + 36. The moving fragments, neutrons, and radiation carry released energy.

4 of 6
Step 3
Follow possible next collisions

Some emitted neutrons cause another fission. A steady chain reaction averages one further fission per fission. More than one makes the rate grow; fewer than one makes it fall. The branching diagram shows possible paths, not a steady operating rate.

5 of 6
Step 4
Account for absorbed and escaped neutrons

Some neutrons escape or are absorbed without causing fission. Control rods absorb neutrons. In many reactors, a moderator slows neutrons so they are more likely to cause fission in the fuel. These are different functions.

6 of 6
Check your understanding

If each fission causes an average of 0.8 further fissions, the rate decreases. Keeping the reaction steady requires an average of 1.

Controlled vs. Uncontrolled Fission

Explore the difference between controlled and uncontrolled fission. Each approach has unique outcomes and risks, shaping how nuclear energy is used in the real world.

CONTROLLED FISSION
UNCONTROLLED FISSION

A steady chain reaction averages one additional fission for each fission. Control rods absorb neutrons; moderators in many reactors slow neutrons. Cooling systems carry heat away. These functions work together, but controlling the reaction rate and removing heat are separate requirements.

This simplified proton–proton chain follows one common route. High temperature, high density, and quantum tunneling allow rare nuclear reactions in the core.

START

Step 1
Form deuterium

Two protons react. One changes into a neutron, producing deuterium (one proton and one neutron), a positron, and an electron neutrino. Two protons do not directly make helium-4.

3 of 6
Step 2
Build helium-3

Deuterium combines with another proton, producing helium-3 (two protons and one neutron) and a gamma photon. The first two stages happen twice to supply two helium-3 nuclei.

4 of 6
Step 3
Make helium-4 and return two protons

Two helium-3 nuclei combine, producing helium-4 and returning two protons. Nucleon counts balance: 3 + 3 = 4 + 1 + 1. Nuclear charge also balances: 2 + 2 = 2 + 1 + 1. Across the whole chain, four protons are used to make one helium-4 nucleus.

Step 4
Account for the released energy

The complete reaction has lower total rest mass in its products. That difference becomes kinetic energy and radiation; neutrinos carry some energy away. Total mass-energy remains conserved.

Mass-Energy: Why Nuclear Changes Release So Much Energy

Compare the total rest mass before and after a nuclear reaction. If the products have less rest mass, the difference Δm corresponds to released energy E = Δmc². Energy is not created or lost. Nuclear binding energy is the energy required to separate a nucleus completely into its free protons and neutrons.

Walkthrough: From nuclear energy to electricity

Trace energy through a simplified reactor with a separate steam loop. Real reactor designs differ.

START
2 of 7
Step 1
Release energy in fission

A fuel nucleus splits. Moving fragments and neutrons, plus radiation, carry released energy. Collisions in the fuel and surrounding material convert much of that energy into thermal energy.

3 of 7
Step 2
Carry heat away from the core

Coolant circulates through the reactor and transfers thermal energy away from the fuel. Cooling is still needed after shutdown because radioactive decay continues to release heat.

4 of 7
Step 3
Transfer energy to a steam loop

In this example, hot coolant transfers energy through a heat exchanger to a separate water loop. The water boils. The two fluids exchange energy without mixing; this is one reactor design, not a feature of every reactor.

5 of 7
Step 4
Use expanding steam to turn a turbine

High-pressure steam expands through turbine blades, doing work and turning the shaft. Some thermal energy becomes mechanical energy. A condenser removes remaining heat so water can circulate again.

6 of 7
Step 5
Drive a generator

The rotating shaft drives a generator. Electromagnetic induction transfers mechanical energy into electrical energy supplied to the circuit. Only part of the original nuclear energy becomes electricity; the rest is transferred mainly as heat to the surroundings.

7 of 7
Check your understanding

Identify each link: nuclear energy release → thermal energy → turbine motion → electrical energy. The coolant transfers energy, the turbine turns, and the generator produces electricity.
