How Do Scientists Decide Which Model to Use?

In science, there are often several ways to explain the same phenomenon. Scientists use models—simplified representations of reality—to make sense of the world. But not every model works for every situation. Choosing the right model depends on the evidence available and the specific question being asked. Understanding how and why scientists select models is key to making sense of discoveries in physics and beyond.

Scientific models are tools that help us explain, predict, and understand complex systems. They are not perfect copies of reality, but rather simplified versions that highlight the most important features for a given situation. A model is considered successful if it matches the evidence and helps us make accurate predictions. Importantly, a model can be useful even if it doesn't explain everything—its value depends on how well it fits the evidence in its intended domain.

Why Evidence Supports Each Model

Understanding the reasoning behind each match helps you see the strengths and limits of every model. Read the explanations to deepen your insight.

Wave Models Explain Interference

Wave models are powerful for explaining phenomena like double-slit interference, diffraction, and polarization. These effects rely on the ability of waves to overlap, combine, and be filtered by orientation.

For example, the alternating bands in double-slit experiments and the behavior of light through polarizing filters are best described by wave properties.

Particle Models Explain Impacts

Particle models are essential for explaining the photoelectric effect and individual photon detections. Only the particle model accounts for why electrons are ejected only above a certain frequency and why light can arrive as discrete packets.

These observations show that light sometimes behaves as if it is made of particles, not just waves.

Nuclear/Probabilistic Models Explain Decay

Nuclear and probabilistic models are needed to explain radioactive decay. The random, statistical nature of decay and the changes in atomic structure require a model that goes beyond waves or particles alone.

This approach helps us predict half-lives and understand nuclear transformations.

Both Models for Some Phenomena

Some observations, like single-photon interference buildup and emission/absorption spectra, require both wave and particle models. These phenomena show evidence of both behaviors depending on how the experiment is set up.

This is why scientists talk about wave-particle duality and use multiple models to fully explain what they see.

Why No Single Model Explains Everything

Every scientific model has its strengths and its limits. While a model might explain certain observations very well, it may fail in other situations. This is why scientists often use more than one model to understand complex phenomena like light and atomic nuclei.

A model is useful because it explains certain evidence—not because it explains everything.

Spectral Lines: What Can They Tell Us?

When scientists look at the light from stars or gases, they see patterns of dark or bright lines called spectra. These lines reveal information about the elements present and whether the source is moving toward or away from us. Spectral evidence is a powerful tool for testing and selecting scientific models.

Worked example: Calculate a spectral shift

A spectral line with a rest wavelength of 500 nm is observed at 505 nm. Find the fractional shift and estimate radial speed using the low-speed Doppler approximation.

START
2 of 6
Step 1
Compare observed and rest wavelengths

The observed wavelength is longer. This is a redshift in the simple motion model. Keep both wavelengths in the same units.

3 of 6
Step 2
Calculate the fractional shift

Divide the change by the rest wavelength. The nanometre units cancel, so redshift z is dimensionless.

4 of 6
Step 3
Estimate radial speed

For speeds small compared with light speed, radial speed is approximately c times z. Use c ≈ 300,000 km/s. The positive sign indicates recession in this convention.

5 of 6
Step 4
Check a second spectral line

All lines share the same fractional shift in this model. A rest line at 600 nm moves to 606 nm, not 605 nm. No detected shift would mean no detected radial motion, not necessarily no sideways motion.

6 of 6
Check the complete result

The source is receding at approximately 3,000 km/s along the line of sight. The approximation is appropriate for this small fractional shift.
