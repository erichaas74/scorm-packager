Unit 5 Lesson 4: Digital Waves and Information Transfer


Teacher Simulation Walkthrough Ideas


Part 1: Waves Carry Information
Learning goal: Explain how information can be encoded, transmitted, received, and reconstructed using waves.


Simulation ideas
• Message-to-wave model: Type a short message or select an image and convert it into a changing signal.
• Sender-to-receiver pathway: Show source, encoder, transmitter, channel, receiver, decoder, and output.
• Information versus energy control: Increase signal amplitude without changing the encoded message.
• Noise injection: Add interference during transmission and compare the original and received message.
• Bandwidth comparison: Send a simple tone, voice clip, and image and compare how much signal variation is needed.
• Wireless example: Follow a phone message through a tower, network, and receiving device.


Prediction and misconception questions
• Does a stronger signal automatically contain more information? Reveal that strength can improve detection without changing the message.
• Does the material of the wave travel from sender to receiver? Reveal that energy and encoded patterns travel.
• Is the message physically stored inside one crest? Reveal that information is represented by a pattern over time.
• If a signal is weaker, must the information be different? Reveal the same encoding at different amplitudes.


Part 2: Digital vs. Analog Signals
Learning goal: Compare continuous analog encoding with discrete digital encoding and evaluate performance in noise.


Simulation ideas
• Analog-versus-digital transmission: Send the same sound through both systems.
• Noise threshold test: Increase interference and compare reconstruction quality.
• Sampling model: Convert a continuous signal into discrete measurements and rebuild it.
• Sampling-rate comparison: Show accurate and inaccurate reconstruction at different rates.
• Quantization model: Compare many possible digital levels with only a few levels.
• Bit-error threshold: Increase noise until 0s and 1s are misread.
• Error correction: Add repeated or check bits and recover a damaged message.
• AM-versus-FM comparison: Encode the same audio through amplitude and frequency modulation and then add noise.


Prediction and misconception questions
• Is a digital signal always a perfectly square physical wave? Reveal that digital describes the encoding, not necessarily the transmitted shape.
• Is digital information perfect no matter how much noise is added? Reveal that bit errors occur after a threshold.
• Is analog always more accurate because it is continuous? Reveal that analog can accumulate noise while digital can be regenerated.
• Does a higher sampling rate always change the original sound? Reveal that it improves representation without changing the source.
• Can a low sampling rate create a false lower frequency? Reveal aliasing.
• Is FM digital because frequency changes? Reveal that AM and FM are both analog modulation methods.


Part 3: Technology Communication Model
Learning goal: Model how a real communication technology uses wave behavior and system components to transfer information.


Simulation ideas
• Clickable communication system using a cell phone, radio, Wi-Fi, fiber optics, or satellite example.
• Fiber-optic model: Encode light pulses, reflect them through a cable, and decode them at the receiver.
• Radio broadcast model: Combine a carrier wave and information signal, transmit it, and demodulate it.
• Satellite communication model: Show uplink, relay, downlink, delay, and signal spreading.
• Screen-versus-printer comparison: Show RGB light production on a screen and CMY pigment printing.
• Failure-point challenge: Disable the encoder, transmitter, channel, receiver, or decoder and predict the result.
• Design tradeoff panel: Compare range, speed, noise resistance, energy use, cost, and bandwidth.


Prediction and misconception questions
• Does a receiver simply copy the original wave exactly? Reveal that it detects and decodes the information pattern.
• Does fiber-optic information move because glass carries light particles like a pipe carries water? Reveal repeated internal reflection and signal propagation.
• Is satellite communication instantaneous? Reveal travel time and processing delay.
• Can every communication problem be solved by increasing power? Reveal limits involving noise, bandwidth, cost, and interference.
• Do screens and printers produce color in the same way? Reveal additive RGB versus subtractive CMY methods.


Recommended teacher walkthrough sequence
1. Encode a short message into a wave signal.
2. Transmit it through a clean channel and reconstruct it.
3. Add noise to an analog signal.
4. Repeat with a digital signal and increase noise to the bit-error threshold.
5. Change sampling rate and reveal aliasing.
6. Compare AM and FM under interference.
7. Trace one complete communication technology from source to output.
8. Introduce a failure or design constraint and evaluate the system response.


Highest-priority counterintuitive reveals
• Digital refers to discrete encoding, not a perfectly square physical wave.
• Digital signals can fail when noise crosses a detection threshold.
• Stronger signals do not automatically carry more information.
• Analog signals can degrade gradually, while digital signals can be regenerated until bit errors occur.
• Screens and printers use different color-mixing systems.
