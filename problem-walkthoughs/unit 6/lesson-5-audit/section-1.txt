Building Blocks: What You Need to Know First

Before we dive deeper, let’s quickly review two key ideas: magnetic fields and electric current. Magnetic fields are invisible regions around magnets where magnetic forces can be felt. Electric current is the flow of electric charge, usually through a wire. These two concepts are the foundation for understanding how moving magnets and wires can work together to produce electricity.

How Changing Fields Create Electricity

When a magnetic field near a wire or coil changes—by moving the magnet, the wire, or both—a voltage is created in the wire. This voltage can push charges, causing an electric current to flow. This is the basic phenomenon of electromagnetic induction, and it’s the principle behind everything from power plants to phone chargers.

What Affects the Induced Current?

Expand each factor below to see how it influences the amount of current produced when a magnetic field changes near a coil or wire.

Strength of Magnetic Field

Induced emf depends on how quickly magnetic flux changes. With the same coil geometry and motion, a stronger magnet can produce a larger flux change in the same time and therefore a larger emf. A strong but unchanging flux produces no induced emf. Current also depends on whether the circuit is closed and on its resistance.

Area of the Coil

For a uniform magnetic field changing at the same rate, a larger loop area gives a larger change in magnetic flux and a larger induced emf, if orientation is unchanged. Increasing area is not enough by itself: the flux must change. A stationary loop in a steady field has no induced emf.

Number of Turns in Coil

More turns of wire in the coil mean the changing magnetic field affects more wire, multiplying the induced voltage.

That’s why generators and transformers use coils with many loops.

Speed of Motion

Moving the magnet or coil faster causes the magnetic field to change more quickly, which increases the induced voltage.

Sliding a magnet quickly through a coil produces a bigger current than moving it slowly.

Orientation of Coil

Magnetic flux depends on the angle between the field and the normal to the coil: Φ = BA cos θ. Induced emf depends on how rapidly that flux changes. For steady rotation, flux is greatest when the normal is parallel to the field, but the instantaneous emf is zero there. The emf magnitude is greatest as flux passes through zero, when the normal is perpendicular to the field.

Faraday’s Law: The Faster the Change, the Greater the Voltage

Faraday’s Law tells us that the amount of voltage induced in a coil depends on how quickly the magnetic field through the coil changes. The faster the magnetic flux changes, the greater the induced voltage. This means that moving a magnet quickly, or using a coil with more loops, can both increase the voltage produced.

Lenz’s Law: Nature Pushes Back

Lenz’s Law helps us predict the direction of the induced current. It states that the induced current will always flow in a direction that opposes the change in magnetic flux that caused it. In other words, nature resists sudden changes—if you try to increase the magnetic field through a coil, the induced current creates its own field to push back against that increase.

Lenz’s Law in Real Life

Expand each scenario to see how Lenz’s Law applies and what happens to the direction of the induced current.

Magnet Entering a Coil

When a magnet moves into a coil, the magnetic flux through the coil increases. The induced current flows in a direction that creates a magnetic field opposing the magnet’s entry.

This means the coil acts like it’s trying to push the magnet back out.

Magnet Leaving a Coil

As the magnet exits the coil, the magnetic flux decreases. The induced current now flows in the opposite direction, creating a field that tries to keep the magnet inside.

The coil “pulls” on the magnet, resisting its departure.

Reversing Magnet Polarity

If you flip the magnet and move it through the coil, the direction of the induced current reverses to oppose the new change in flux.

This is why the direction of current depends on both the motion and the orientation of the magnet.

Conservation of Energy

Lenz’s Law ensures that energy is conserved. The induced current always acts to oppose the motion, so you have to do work to keep the magnet moving.

This is why it feels harder to push a magnet into a coil when current is flowing.

Worked example: Calculate an induced voltage

A 100-turn coil has area 0.020 m² per turn. A uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the coil grows from 0.10 T to 0.30 T in 0.50 s. Find average induced EMF. The complete circuit has resistance 10 Ω.

START
2 of 6
Step 1
Calculate flux change per turn

Magnetic flux is field times area when the field is perpendicular to the coil. The given area is for one turn, so do not multiply by the turn count yet.

3 of 6
Step 2
Apply Faraday’s law

Average EMF magnitude equals the turn count times flux change per turn divided by the elapsed time.

4 of 6
Step 3
Use Lenz’s law for direction

An increasing field into the page induces a field out of the page. The right-hand rule gives counterclockwise current as viewed here. For negligible self-inductance, divide EMF by total resistance.

5 of 6
Step 4
Test the rate of change

The same flux change in half the time produces twice the average EMF. Once the field becomes steady, induced EMF from this changing external field is zero.

6 of 6
Check the complete result

Average induced EMF is 0.80 V and the resistive-circuit current is 0.080 A. Induced current opposes the change in flux, not necessarily the existing field.
