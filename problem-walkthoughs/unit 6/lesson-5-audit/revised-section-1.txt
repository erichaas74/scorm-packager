Lighting Up the World with Moving Magnets

Move a magnet toward a closed wire coil and a current can flow. Hold it still and the induced current stops. The key is changing magnetic flux through the coil, not simply having a magnet nearby.

Building Blocks: What You Need to Know First

A magnetic field describes magnetic influences around magnets and currents. Electric current is the flow of charge. Induction connects these ideas: changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an electromotive force (emf), measured in volts. Current also requires a conducting path.

How Changing Fields Create Electricity

Induced emf depends on how quickly magnetic flux through a circuit changes. Moving a magnet toward a coil can change that flux. Motion alone is not enough: translating a coil within a uniform, steady field without changing its area or orientation leaves its flux unchanged.

Faraday’s Law: The Faster the Change, the Greater the Voltage

Faraday’s law compares flux change with elapsed time. For the same turn count, a larger flux change or a shorter time gives a larger average emf. For the same flux change per turn and time, more turns give a larger emf.

Induced Voltage Appears

A faster change in flux produces a larger emf for the same coil. Reversing the magnet’s motion reverses the emf polarity. An open circuit can have induced voltage but no sustained conduction current.

5 of 6
Step 4
Current Flows in Circuit

In a closed conducting circuit, the emf drives current. For a resistive circuit with negligible self-inductance, current equals emf divided by total resistance. Opening the circuit interrupts this current.

6 of 6
Summary of Electromagnetic Induction

Changing flux induces emf. A closed path allows current. A stationary magnet, or a coil translated wholly within a uniform steady field at fixed orientation and area, produces no emf from a change in flux.

Lenz’s Law: Nature Pushes Back

Lenz’s law gives the direction: induced current creates a magnetic field that opposes the change in flux. It opposes an increase by producing field in the opposite direction, and opposes a decrease by producing field in the original direction. This applies to slow as well as rapid changes.

Worked example: Calculate an induced voltage

A 100-turn coil has area 0.020 m² per turn. Viewed face-on, a uniform field points into the page, perpendicular to the coil, and grows from 0.10 T to 0.30 T in 0.50 s. Find average induced emf and current direction. The closed circuit has total resistance 10 Ω and negligible self-inductance.

START
2 of 6
Step 1
Calculate flux change per turn

Magnetic flux is field times area when the field is perpendicular to the coil. The given area is for one turn, so do not multiply by the turn count yet.

3 of 6
Step 2
Apply Faraday’s law

Average EMF magnitude equals the turn count times flux change per turn divided by the elapsed time.

4 of 6
Step 3
Use Lenz’s law for direction

An increasing field into the page induces a field out of the page. The right-hand rule gives counterclockwise current as viewed here. For negligible self-inductance, divide EMF by total resistance.

5 of 6
Step 4
Test the rate of change

The same flux change in half the time produces twice the average EMF. Once the field becomes steady, induced EMF from this changing external field is zero.

Check the complete result

Average induced EMF is 0.80 V and the resistive-circuit current is 0.080 A. Induced current opposes the change in flux, not necessarily the existing field.
