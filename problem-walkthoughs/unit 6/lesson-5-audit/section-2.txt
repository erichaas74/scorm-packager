Review: Key Concepts You Need

Before we dive in, let's quickly review some important ideas. Electric current is the flow of electric charge, and magnetic fields are invisible regions around magnets or current-carrying wires where magnetic forces act. When a current flows through a wire in a magnetic field, it experiences a force—this is the foundation for how motors and generators work.

How Magnetic Force Drives Motion

The magic behind motors and generators starts with a simple principle: when an electric current flows through a wire placed in a magnetic field, the wire feels a force. This force can make the wire move, spin, or turn, depending on how it's arranged. This basic effect is the key to transforming energy from one form to another in countless devices.

What Affects Motor Performance?

Expand each section to learn how different factors influence how well a motor works. Understanding these can help you design or improve motors for various uses.

Number of Coil Turns

More turns increase the torque on a motor coil when current, magnetic field, coil area, and orientation are held constant. Adding turns also changes wire length and resistance, so a real motor does not automatically gain speed or output power. Compare the conditions before predicting the result.

Current Strength

A larger current gives greater torque when the field, coil geometry, and orientation are unchanged. The resulting speed depends on the load and the motor’s operating conditions. Increasing current also increases resistive heating for a fixed resistance.

Magnetic Field Strength

A stronger magnetic field increases torque when current and coil geometry are held constant. It does not automatically make the motor more efficient: efficiency depends on useful output compared with total input, including electrical and mechanical losses.

Coil Orientation

Motor torque depends on the angle θ between the coil’s normal and the magnetic field: τ = NIAB sin θ. Torque is zero when the normal is parallel to the field and greatest when the normal is perpendicular to the field. The commutator reverses current each half-turn in a simple DC motor so the torque continues to drive rotation in the same direction.

Commutator Function

The commutator ensures the current reverses at the right time, keeping the coil spinning in one direction. A faulty commutator can cause jerky or stopped motion.

Turning Motion Into Electricity: Generators

Electric generators work in the opposite way to motors. Instead of using electricity to create motion, they use motion—like spinning a coil—to generate electricity. This process is at the heart of how power plants, wind turbines, and even hand-crank flashlights produce the electricity we use every day.

What Affects Generator Output?

Expand each section to discover how different factors influence the amount of electricity a generator can produce. These variables are key to designing efficient generators.

Rotation Speed

Faster rotation of the coil increases the rate of change of magnetic flux, resulting in a higher output voltage. Wind turbines generate more electricity on windy days because they spin faster.

Magnetic Field Strength

A stronger magnetic field induces a greater voltage in the coil. Using powerful magnets boosts generator output.

Number of Coil Turns

For the same flux change per turn, more coil turns give a larger induced emf. The current delivered to a load also depends on total resistance and operating conditions. More turns do not automatically produce a proportionally larger current.

Coil Area

Larger coils capture more of the magnetic field, leading to greater voltage generation. This is why large generators have big coils.

Type of Current Produced (AC/DC)

Generators can be designed to produce alternating current (AC) or direct current (DC), depending on their construction and intended use.

Efficiency and Energy Losses: The Real World

Motors and generators are powerful, but they're not perfect. Some of the energy put into these devices is lost as heat, sound, or friction—like leaks in a pipeline. Understanding where these losses come from helps engineers design more efficient machines and save energy in everyday life.

Where Does the Energy Go?

Expand each section to learn about common sources of energy loss in motors and generators, and how engineers try to reduce them.

Electrical Resistance

Wires and coils resist the flow of current, causing some energy to turn into heat. Using thicker wires or better materials can help reduce this loss.

Friction

Moving parts, like bearings and brushes, rub against each other and waste energy as heat and sound. Lubrication and careful design can minimize friction.

Heat Production

Both resistance and friction generate heat, which can make devices warm or even hot. Cooling systems or heat sinks are often used to manage this.

Sound Energy

Some energy is lost as noise when parts move or vibrate. While not always significant, it can be reduced with quieter designs.

Eddy Currents

Changing magnetic fields can induce unwanted currents in metal parts, wasting energy as heat. Laminated cores or special materials help reduce eddy current losses.
