    - heading "What Makes Series and Parallel Circuits Different?" [level=2]:
      - generic: What Makes Series and Parallel Circuits Different?
    - paragraph: Series and parallel circuits might look similar at first glance, but the way their components are connected makes a huge difference. In a series circuit, everything is linked in a single loop, so the same current flows through each device. In a parallel circuit, each device has its own direct path to the power source, allowing current to split and travel through multiple branches. These differences affect how energy is shared, how devices perform, and what happens if something goes wrong.
    - heading "Understanding Circuit Relationships" [level=2]:
      - generic: Understanding Circuit Relationships
    - paragraph: Compare the diagrams below. In a series loop, steady current is the same through each component and voltage drops add to the supply voltage. Across parallel branches, voltage is the same and branch currents add to the total. Charge circulates without being consumed; the source transfers energy to the devices and surroundings. For a device, power P = VI and energy transferred E = Pt.

    - heading "How Does Resistance Add Up?" [level=2]:
      - generic: How Does Resistance Add Up?
    - paragraph: Resistance is like a traffic jam for electric current—it slows down the flow. But how resistance adds up depends on how you arrange the components. In a series circuit, adding more resistors makes it harder for current to flow, just like making a pipe longer. In a parallel circuit, adding more branches actually makes it easier for current to flow, like adding more lanes to a highway. Understanding how to calculate total (equivalent) resistance is key to predicting how your circuit will behave.
