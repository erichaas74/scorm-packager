A
Ammeter — always in SERIES

Built with almost no resistance: RA ≈ 0.1 Ω

✔
Current is what flows through a point, so the meter has to sit in the path and let that same current pass through it. Its tiny resistance barely adds to the loop:
I = V ÷ (Rbulb + RA) = 9 V ÷ 100.1 Ω = 0.090 A
Without the meter the current would be 0.090 A. The meter reduces it by about 0.1%, so the rounded readings look the same. This is small but nonzero meter loading.
✘
Wired in parallel it creates a very low-resistance branch across the bulb. With the model's ideal 9 V source, the meter-branch current would be:
I = V ÷ RA = 9 V ÷ 0.1 Ω = 90 A
This is a short-circuit fault. Current divides between branches; it does not all skip the bulb. The ideal-source calculation ignores battery and wire resistance and protection. A real fault may lower terminal voltage, blow a fuse, overheat conductors, or damage the meter. Do not build this connection.
V
Voltmeter — always in PARALLEL

Built with a huge resistance: RV ≈ 10 MΩ

✔
Voltage is the difference between two points, so the meter has to touch both ends of the bulb at once — that is what parallel means. Its huge resistance means it barely steals any current:
I = V ÷ RV = 9 V ÷ 10,000,000 Ω = 0.0000009 A
That is about 1/100,000 of the bulb's 0.090 A, so the circuit hardly notices it is being measured.
✘
Wired in series its huge resistance takes over the whole loop:
I = V ÷ (Rbulb + RV) = 9 V ÷ 10,000,100 Ω ≈ 0.0000009 A
The current collapses to almost nothing and the bulb stays dark. Nearly all 9 V is dropped across the meter itself, so it reads about 9 V while measuring nothing useful.
