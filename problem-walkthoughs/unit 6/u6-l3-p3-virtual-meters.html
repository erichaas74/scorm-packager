A Ammeter — always in SERIES

Built with almost no resistance: RA ≈ 0.1 Ω

✔
Current is what flows through a point, so the meter has to sit in the path and let that same current pass through it. Its tiny resistance barely adds to the loop: I = V ÷ (Rbulb + RA) = 9 V ÷ 100.1 Ω = 0.090 A Without the meter the current would be 9 ÷ 100 = 0.090 A — the meter changes it by only 0.1%, so what it reads is the real current.
✘
Wired in parallel it lays a near-zero-resistance path straight across the bulb. Ohm's law with a tiny R gives a monstrous current: I = V ÷ RA = 9 V ÷ 0.1 Ω = 90 A That is a short circuit — the current skips the bulb and wrecks the meter.

V Voltmeter — always in PARALLEL

Built with a huge resistance: RV ≈ 10 MΩ

✔
Voltage is the difference between two points, so the meter has to touch both ends of the bulb at once — that is what parallel means. Its huge resistance means it barely steals any current: I = V ÷ RV = 9 V ÷ 10,000,000 Ω = 0.0000009 A That is about 1/100,000 of the bulb's 0.090 A, so the circuit hardly notices it is being measured.
✘
Wired in series its huge resistance takes over the whole loop: I = V ÷ (Rbulb + RV) = 9 V ÷ 10,000,100 Ω ≈ 0.0000009 A The current collapses to almost nothing and the bulb stays dark. Nearly all 9 V is dropped across the meter itself, so it reads about 9 V while measuring nothing useful.
