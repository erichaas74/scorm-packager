How Do Electric Charges Interact Without Touching?

Have you ever wondered how a balloon can stick to a wall or why your hair stands up after removing a sweater? These everyday mysteries are examples of electric charges interacting without ever touching. In physics, this phenomenon is called action-at-a-distance. But how can objects influence each other when there’s nothing visible connecting them? The answer lies in the concept of electric fields, which help us model and understand these invisible forces at work.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to achieve the following learning objectives related to electric fields:

2

Understanding Electric Field Direction: The Role of the Test Charge

The direction of an electric field is always defined by the force it would exert on a positive test charge. If you place a small, positive test charge near another charged object (the source charge), the direction the test charge moves shows the field’s direction. It’s important to distinguish between the source charge, which creates the field, and the test charge, which is used to detect the field’s effects. This convention helps us consistently describe and draw electric fields.

Electric Field Lines: How They Behave

Explore how electric fields behave around different charges. Expand each section to see how field lines are drawn and what they mean for positive and negative charges.

Field Lines from a Positive Charge

Field lines around a positive charge radiate outward in all directions. This shows that a positive test charge placed nearby would be pushed away from the source charge.

Imagine the sun’s rays spreading out from the center—field lines act similarly for positive charges.

Field Lines from a Negative Charge

Field lines around a negative charge point inward, toward the charge. This means a positive test charge would be pulled toward the negative source charge.

Think of water flowing down a drain—field lines for negative charges converge inward in the same way.

Field Lines Never Cross

Field lines never cross. At any point where the net electric field is nonzero, it has one direction. Crossing lines would incorrectly assign two directions to that one field. Where the net field is zero, its direction is undefined.

What Makes an Electric Field Strong or Weak?

Electric field strength tells us how powerful the field is at a specific location. The closer you are to the source charge, the stronger the field becomes. As you move farther away, the field’s strength decreases rapidly. In diagrams, field strength is often shown by how close together the field lines are: the denser the lines, the stronger the field at that spot.

What Happens When There’s More Than One Charge?

In real-life situations, electric fields often come from more than one charge. When this happens, the fields from each charge combine to create a net electric field. To find the total effect, we use vector addition, which means adding the direction and strength of each field together. This helps us predict how a test charge will move in complex arrangements.

Worked example: Find a field and a charge’s force

A +4.0 μC source charge is 0.30 m left of point P. A small −2.0 nC charge is placed at P. Calculate the electric field at P and the force on that negative charge.

START
2 of 6
Step 1
Separate source from test charge

The source charge determines the field. At P, the field points right, away from the positive source. A negative charge feels a force opposite to the field.

3 of 6
Step 2
Calculate field strength

Use the source charge and its distance from P. Field strength is force per unit positive test charge.

4 of 6
Step 3
Calculate the signed force

Take right as positive and multiply by the signed charge. A negative result reverses direction relative to the field.

5 of 6
Step 4
Check what changes with distance

At twice the distance the source field is one quarter as strong, so the same test charge experiences one quarter the force. Force determines acceleration, not necessarily existing velocity.

6 of 6
Check the complete result

The field is 4.00 × 10⁵ N/C rightward. The negative charge experiences about 7.99 × 10⁻⁴ N leftward.
