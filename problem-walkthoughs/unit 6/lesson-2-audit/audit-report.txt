3. NEGATIVE-CHARGE WORK QUESTION CONFUSES FIELD DIRECTION WITH FORCE AND WORK.
Location: section 2, 'If you move a negative charge toward higher potential, are you doing work with or against the electric field?'
The key accepts 'Against the field' and the feedback says the learner is working against the electric field. The displacement is indeed opposite E, but the electric force on a negative charge is in the direction of that displacement. For q < 0 and ΔV > 0, ΔU = qΔV < 0 and W_E = -ΔU > 0. External work depends on how motion is controlled; at constant speed it is negative. Replace the ambiguous question with separate predictions for force direction, ΔU, and electric work. The negative-charge accordion already explains this correctly.

4. GRAVITATIONAL POTENTIAL ENERGY IS NOT 'ALWAYS POSITIVE'.
Location: section 2 sorting activity, Compare Electric and Gravitational Potential Energy.
The cards 'Always positive' and 'Can be positive or negative' do not create a valid distinction between the two types of potential energy. Both depend on the chosen reference; gravitational potential energy can be negative. Replace these cards with meaningful contrasts and shared properties, such as ΔU_g = mgΔh, ΔU_e = qΔV, dependence on position, and energy transfer by a conservative force. A comparison table or categories Electric / Gravitational / Both would work better.

5. INDUCTION PROCEDURE MISSES THE CRITICAL ORDER.
Location: section 3 charging introduction and Charging by Contact and Induction Process, step 2.
The text conflates temporary polarization with net charging and does not say to disconnect the ground before removing the inducing rod. Teach an explicit sequence: bring rod near neutral conductor; connect ground; disconnect ground while rod remains; remove rod. Show electron motion and the final charge at every stage. Merely bringing a rod near an isolated neutral conductor does not change its net charge. With the rod still nearby, grounding does not necessarily leave the conductor neutral.

6. FIELD DIRECTION IS FORCE DIRECTION, NOT NECESSARILY MOTION.
Location: section 1 Electric Field Direction flashcard, Role of the Test Charge paragraph, first MC feedback; section 2 introductory prose and positive-charge MC.
Define E as force per unit positive test charge. A charge's force and acceleration need not point along its current velocity. The newer worked example correctly states this, but earlier text says the field shows where a charge would move or its path. Add 'released from rest with only the electric force acting' when predicting initial motion. This distinction is essential for the assigned curved-path lab. A small test charge should have negligible influence on the source arrangement, rather than literally no influence.

7. CONDUCTOR FEEDBACK OVERGENERALIZES UNIFORM SURFACE CHARGE.
Location: section 3 metal-sphere MC and Surface Charge and Charge Concentration prose.
The key is reasonable for an isolated spherical conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, but the feedback incorrectly extends uniform distribution to conductors generally. Specify an isolated sphere with no nearby external charges. Replace 'always' statements with the electrostatic-equilibrium condition and distinguish general conducting surfaces from an isolated sphere. The Conductors accordion already makes the correct distinction.

8. LIGHTNING QUESTION TESTS A MISLEADING SHORTCUT.
Location: section 3 'Why are lightning rods pointed?' and its feedback.
The keyed answer implies pointed shape safely routes lightning by itself. The useful safety mechanism is the complete bonded conducting path and grounding system, as the revised Lightning Rods accordion correctly explains. Replace with a question comparing a properly bonded system with an unconnected rod. Replace implausible distractors about birds, rain, and looking impressive with physics-based misconceptions. Do not imply a pointed tip is what guarantees protection.

DEFINITIONS AND EXPLANATION REPAIRS
9. Voltage flashcard omits 'per unit charge' in its explanation of available energy. State 1 V = 1 J/C and distinguish ΔV from ΔU. Define work as energy transferred by a force through displacement, not only energy needed to move a charge against a field. Keep the existing worked distinction W_E = -ΔU and, for constant speed, W_ext = ΔU.
10. Scope the inverse-square statement to a fixed point charge (or the appropriate external spherical-source model). The general claim that moving farther always weakens an electric field clashes with the nearby uniform-field examples. Show a direct comparison between a point charge and the approximately uniform region between large parallel plates.
11. The 'plastic rulers never' cause a shock claim is too absolute. Insulators can acquire static charge and discharge under suitable conditions. Describe restricted charge mobility rather than immunity to static effects. Grounding can supply or remove electrons; it is not solely charge flowing away.
12. Polarization explanations should state why the closer opposite charge produces a stronger attraction in a nonuniform field. The image alt text already contains that useful reasoning, but the visible prose mostly repeats that charges rearrange. Reduce the repeated polarization introduction and make this causal explanation explicit.

TARGETED WORKED EXAMPLES AND PRACTICE TO ADD
A. Perpendicular net field: E1 = 3.0 N/C east, E2 = 4.0 N/C north. Draw components, add tip-to-tail, find E_net = 5.0 N/C at 53.1 degrees north of east, then reverse force direction for a negative test charge. Use different numbers from the assigned lab if this becomes a worked example; for example 6 and 8 N/C give 10 N/C.
B. Negative charge and work: q = -2.0 μC moves from 20 V to 80 V. ΔV = +60 V; ΔU = -1.2 × 10^-4 J; W_E = +1.2 × 10^-4 J; constant-speed W_ext = -1.2 × 10^-4 J. Show E, force, displacement, and energy bars separately.
C. Independent field/force check: Q = +2.0 μC, r = 0.20 m, q = -3.0 nC. E ≈ 4.50 × 10^5 N/C away from Q; force magnitude ≈ 1.35 × 10^-3 N toward Q.
D. Uniform-field check: ΔV magnitude 90 V over 0.030 m gives E = 3000 N/C. Ask for ΔU when +4.0 μC moves from 0 V to 90 V: +3.6 × 10^-4 J.
E. Plate-separation comparison: double d, first at fixed Q and area (E unchanged, voltage doubles), then with a battery maintaining fixed voltage (E halves). Specify ideal large plates and neglect edge effects.
F. Grounding-order challenge: choose the sequence that leaves a conductor charged, then predict what happens if the rod is removed while the ground stays connected.
G. Trajectory prediction: give a charge an initial sideways velocity, show E and q, and ask which way its velocity starts changing. Contrast the path with a field line. Connect directly to the assigned barrier-navigation lab.
