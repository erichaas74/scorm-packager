Connecting Electric Fields and Potential: Why Direction Matters

Electric fields have both direction and strength, but how do these relate to electric potential? The direction of the electric field shows where a positive charge would naturally move—from higher to lower electric potential. Positive charges are pushed toward lower potential, while negative charges move toward higher potential. Understanding this relationship is key to predicting how charges behave in different situations.

Explore how potential and field direction interact.

Movement of Positive Test Charges

A positive charge experiences an electric force in the direction of the electric field, toward lower electric potential. If released from rest and acted on only by the electric force, it accelerates that way. Its electric potential energy decreases while its kinetic energy increases.

Movement of Negative Test Charges

A negative charge experiences an electric force opposite the electric field, toward higher electric potential. If released from rest and acted on only by the electric force, it accelerates that way. Because its charge is negative, moving to higher potential decreases its electric potential energy: U = qV. The field does positive work and its kinetic energy increases.

High vs. Low Electric Potential

Electric potential V is energy per unit charge; electric potential energy U belongs to a charge in a field and depends on its sign: U = qV. A positive charge released from rest accelerates toward lower V, while a negative charge accelerates toward higher V. In both cases, the electric force lowers potential energy and increases kinetic energy when no other force does work.

Energy in Electric Fields: Potential Energy and Work

When a charge moves in an electric field, energy is transferred. This energy can be stored as electric potential energy or released as kinetic energy. Moving a charge with or against the field involves doing work—either the field does work on the charge, or you must do work to move it. Understanding these energy changes helps explain how batteries, capacitors, and even lightning work.

If you want to move the charge back toward the high-potential plate, you must apply a force against the electric field. This requires work, increasing the charge’s potential energy as it moves to a higher potential.

Electric Potential in Action: Parallel Plates and Energy Storage

Parallel plate capacitors are classic devices for exploring electric potential and energy storage. When two plates are separated by a gap and charged, a uniform electric field forms between them. The separation distance and the amount of charge affect the potential difference (voltage) and the energy stored in the field. Electric potential maps, showing equipotential lines, help visualize how potential changes across the space between plates.

In a parallel plate capacitor, two flat plates are placed close together with a gap between them. When charged, a uniform electric field forms in the space between the plates, and the potential difference (voltage) is the same across any line perpendicular to the plates.

This setup is used to store energy in the electric field. The amount of energy stored depends on the charge on the plates and the voltage between them. Capacitors are found in many electronic devices for this reason.

Worked example: Connect voltage, field, and work

Parallel plates are 0.040 m apart. The left plate is at 120 V and the right plate at 0 V. A +2.0 μC charge moves from right to left at constant speed. Neglect edge effects and other forces.

Determine field direction and size

Electric field points from higher to lower potential, so it points right. Use the voltage difference magnitude divided by plate separation for a uniform field.

3 of 6
Step 2
Find the electric force

The positive charge feels a rightward electric force. To move it left at constant speed, an external agent supplies an equal leftward force.

4 of 6
Step 3
Calculate potential-energy change

Use final minus initial potential. The positive charge moves to higher potential and gains electric potential energy.

5 of 6
Step 4
Distinguish the two kinds of work

Electric work is negative because electric force opposes displacement. At constant speed, the external agent supplies the positive energy increase.

The external agent supplies 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ J, stored as electric potential energy. The electric field does the same magnitude of negative work.
