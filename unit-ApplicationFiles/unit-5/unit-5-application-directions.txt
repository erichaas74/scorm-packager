U5L2

DESIGN THE PERFECT INSTRUMENT
UNIT 5 BUZZ LAB GUIDE

YOUR MISSION
Step into the role of an instrument engineer! You will tune strings and air columns, test which standing-wave modes can exist, and discover how musicians control pitch through length, temperature, and boundary conditions.

WHAT THE LAB WILL SHOW
- Adjustable strings and air columns model standing waves. The tuner shows how closely your design matches the challenge's target frequency.
- Changing the vibrating length or selected mode changes pitch. Air temperature also changes sound speed and the resonant frequencies of air columns.
- Strings fixed at both ends and pipes open at both ends support whole-number harmonics. A stopped pipe has different end conditions and supports only odd harmonics in this model.
- Recorded builds show how length limits, allowed modes, and boundary conditions constrain a design. Some targets cannot be reached within the allowed settings.

WHAT YOU WILL DO AND LEARN
- Relate resonator length, wave speed, harmonic mode, and frequency.
- Compare strings, open pipes, and stopped pipes using their boundary conditions.
- Defend an engineering choice with evidence from successful and impossible builds.

HOW TO COMPLETE THE LAB
1. Work through all seven design challenges and read each target frequency and constraint carefully.
2. Adjust the resonator, mode, temperature, and length or capo position until the tuner confirms a valid build.
3. For the impossible case, use No mode fits only after checking every allowed mode.
4. Use Your recorded builds to answer the Buzz questions in order and cite specific builds in the final two responses.
5. Download the evidence image only if you want a personal record; it is not submitted or graded.
6. Before submitting, confirm that you answered all 8 questions and that the final two responses cite your recorded evidence.

RESONANCE TUBE LAB
UNIT 5 BUZZ LAB GUIDE

YOUR MISSION
Turn an adjustable air column into a precision sound detector! You will hunt for resonance peaks, uncover the odd-harmonic pattern inside a closed tube, and use your own measurements to calculate the speed of sound.

WHAT THE LAB WILL SHOW
- A tuning fork drives an air column that is closed by water at the bottom and open at the top. Changing the air-column length produces peaks in the intensity trace when the tube resonates.
- The first three resonances correspond to the 1st, 3rd, and 5th harmonics. A displacement node at the water and an antinode at the open end allow odd numbers of quarter wavelengths to fit.
- Higher-frequency forks resonate at shorter air-column lengths. Compare the required lengths across all four forks.
- Your measurements let you calculate wavelength = 4L/n and wave speed = frequency x wavelength. Comparing the calculated speeds tests whether changing frequency changes sound speed in the same air conditions.

WHAT YOU WILL DO AND LEARN
- Identify resonance by connecting tube length, frequency, and sound intensity.
- Explain why a closed-open tube supports odd harmonics.
- Use repeated measurements to determine whether sound speed depends on frequency.

HOW TO COMPLETE THE LAB
1. Strike a tuning fork and scan the tube length until the intensity trace reaches a resonance peak.
2. Record the required resonance lengths for 2048 Hz, 1024 Hz, 512 Hz, and 256 Hz until all 12 rows are complete.
3. Use Questions 1-4 to convert the three lengths for each frequency, calculate wavelengths and speeds, and report each frequency average.
4. Use Questions 5-6 for the overall average and percent difference, then answer the remaining questions using your recorded lengths and calculated speeds.
5. The progress bar tracks the 12 measurements; submit all calculations and explanations in Buzz.
6. Before submitting, confirm that you answered all 10 questions and that the final two responses cite your recorded evidence.

UNIT 5 BUZZ LAB GUIDE

Build color the way light and your eyes do! You will filter wavelengths, mix red-green-blue light, and compare cone responses to explain why the same color sensation can come from very different light combinations.

WHAT THE LAB WILL SHOW

- Spectra show which wavelengths are present, while cone-response displays model the relative responses of the eye's three cone types.

THIN LENS INVESTIGATION
UNIT 5 BUZZ LAB GUIDE

YOUR MISSION
Make light rays reveal an invisible rule! With the object fixed for each trial, move the screen around a converging lens, observe real and virtual images, and test whether the thin-lens equation predicts what the simulation shows.

WHAT THE LAB WILL SHOW
- Four canvases place an object at different fixed distances from a converging lens. Moving each screen lets you look for a focused real image.
- Ray diagrams show how object distance affects image position, size, and orientation. A real image forms where transmitted rays meet and can be focused on a screen.
- When the object is inside the focal length, the emerging rays diverge and their backward extensions locate a virtual image. Moving the screen cannot capture that virtual image.
- Measurements from the real-image trials let you determine the common focal length using 1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i. Use that focal length to infer the virtual-image distance and magnification in L4, following the assessment's sign convention.

WHAT YOU WILL DO AND LEARN
- Distinguish real images from virtual images using ray behavior and screen evidence.
- Apply the thin-lens equation and magnification relationship with correct signs.
- Use multiple trials to explain how object distance changes image position, size, and orientation.

HOW TO COMPLETE THE LAB

2. Answer image height, signed image distance, focal length, magnification, and orientation directly below each canvas. For L4 infer virtual-image values using the focal length found from L1-L3.
3. Write the final conclusion about the common focal length and real versus virtual images.
4. Use the four trials as numerical and operational evidence in your conclusion.
5. Submit all chart entries and written responses through Buzz; no downloads or separate notebook are required.

SPECTRAL SHIFT INVESTIGATION
UNIT 5 BUZZ LAB GUIDE

YOUR MISSION
Read motion written in starlight! You will match elemental fingerprints, slide spectral lines into alignment, and use wavelength shifts to decide whether distant objects are racing toward us or away from us.

WHAT THE LAB WILL SHOW
- Reference spectra display characteristic patterns of elemental spectral lines. Compare those patterns with the observed spectrum to identify the matching element.
- The alignment markers reveal how far the observed lines have shifted from their rest wavelengths.
- A shift toward longer wavelengths is a redshift; a shift toward shorter wavelengths is a blueshift. In this investigation, these shifts indicate motion away from or toward the observer along the line of sight.
- The measured shift and rest wavelength let you calculate z = (observed wavelength - rest wavelength)/rest wavelength and approximate radial velocity using v = cz for small shifts. Compare all three objects using both the sign and size of the shift.

WHAT YOU WILL DO AND LEARN
- Identify an element from its unique spectral-line pattern.
- Distinguish redshift from blueshift and connect each to radial motion.
- Calculate fractional wavelength shift and approximate radial speed from measured data.

HOW TO COMPLETE THE LAB
1. Select a reference spectrum with Enter or Space, click it, or drag it into the comparison area to identify the matching elemental fingerprint.
2. Use the marker slider with arrow keys and select Check alignment, or drag the dashed markers until the observed and reference lines align.
3. Record the measured wavelength shift for all three objects and note each direction of motion.
4. Answer the Buzz questions in order, calculating from the displayed rest wavelength and measured shift rather than a displayed z value.
5. Download the evidence image only if you want a personal record; it is not submitted or graded.

GPS AND RELATIVITY INVESTIGATION
UNIT 5 BUZZ LAB GUIDE

YOUR MISSION
Save a GPS receiver from drifting kilometers off course! You will measure how quickly a tiny clock error becomes a navigation failure, separate gravity and motion effects, and engineer the correction that keeps satellites synchronized with Earth.

WHAT THE LAB WILL SHOW
- The timing-error model connects a small clock error to a distance error using distance error = speed of light x timing error.
- Separate displays show the gravitational and motion effects on a GPS satellite clock relative to a ground clock. At GPS orbit, gravity makes the satellite clock run faster and motion makes it run slower; the net effect is a faster clock.
- The oscillator adjustment lets you compensate for the net clock-rate difference and observe the remaining drift.
- Corrected and uncorrected mission runs show how timing errors accumulate into navigation errors. Compare the final distance error with the 10.0 m requirement as well as checking the calibration indicator; an accepted calibration can still leave residual error.

WHAT YOU WILL DO AND LEARN
- Convert a position-accuracy requirement into an allowable timing error.
- Combine gravitational and special-relativistic clock-rate changes for GPS orbit.
- Explain how a pre-launch oscillator correction prevents accumulating navigation error.

HOW TO COMPLETE THE LAB
1. Complete the five-step explainer, then open the four-part investigation.
2. In Part A, find and record the timing error corresponding to the 10.0 m navigation limit.
3. In Part B, record the gravitational-only, motion-only, and combined GPS clock drifts.
4. In Part C, adjust the oscillator correction until residual drift is below 0.2 microseconds per day; then run the corrected and uncorrected missions in Part D.
5. Answer the Buzz questions in order and use results from all four parts in the final responses. The optional evidence image is not submitted or graded.

UNIT 5 BUZZ LAB GUIDE

Put Einstein’s clocks into orbit! You will make gravity and motion compete, locate an orbit where their effects cancel, and explain why satellite navigation depends on corrections measured in millionths of a second.

- A higher altitude contributes a faster clock rate through the gravitational effect, while orbital motion contributes a slower rate. The balance determines whether the orbiting clock gains or loses time.
- The graph and Snap to circular orbit control let you explore how these competing effects vary along circular orbits and where their net drift cancels in the model.
- Recorded fast-clock and slow-clock trials connect the sign and size of the net drift to the selected conditions. The navigation-error comparison shows why even microseconds of drift matter.

WHAT YOU WILL DO AND LEARN
- Compare gravitational and special-relativistic clock-rate changes.
- Determine when an orbiting clock runs faster or slower than a clock on Earth.
- Connect tiny timing drifts to large navigation errors and real engineering decisions.
