4 · Other places relativity shows up

GPS is the most useful test, but it is far from the only one. Question 5 onward uses these cases.

Motion

Cosmic-ray muons

Muons created ~15 km up live only 2.2 µs at rest — long enough to travel about 660 m. Yet they reach the ground in large numbers because at 0.998c their internal clock is stretched.

γ ≈ 15.8, so the lab-frame lifetime becomes ~35 µs (~10 km of travel).

Both

Hafele–Keating, 1971

Four cesium clocks flew around the world on commercial jets. Eastward flights move with Earth's spin (faster in the Earth-centered frame), westward flights move against it and are actually slower than the ground.

Measured: eastward −59 ns, westward +273 ns — both matched prediction.

Gravity

Pound–Rebka tower, 1960

Gamma rays climbed a 22.5 m tower at Harvard. Climbing out of the gravitational well cost them energy, so they arrived redshifted — a clock at the top ticks faster than one at the bottom.

Fractional shift 2.46 × 10⁻¹⁵ — about 0.2 ns per day.

Gravity

White dwarf Sirius B

Sirius B packs a solar mass into an Earth-sized ball, so its surface gravity is ~350 000 g. Light leaving it is redshifted, and its surface clocks run measurably slow compared with ours.

Gravitational redshift ~ 3 × 10⁻⁴ — roughly 25 s per day.

Motion

Storage-ring muons

At CERN, muons circulating at γ = 29.3 lived 29.3 times longer than muons at rest — time dilation confirmed to better than 0.1 % at extreme acceleration.

Rest lifetime 2.2 µs → measured 64.4 µs.

Both

Optical clocks on a lab bench

Modern aluminum-ion clocks are so precise that raising one by 33 cm, or moving it at 10 m/s, produces a measurable change in tick rate. Relativity is now a desktop effect, not just an astronomical one.

33 cm of height → ~4 × 10⁻¹⁷ fractional rate change.
